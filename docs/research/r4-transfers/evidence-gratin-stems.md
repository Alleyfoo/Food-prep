# Evidence: potato–rutabaga gratin (A) and broccoli-stem patties (B)

Date read: 2026-11-24 (all entries). Read how: every page below was read through WebFetch, a summarising fetch tool. It returns a model-written summary of the page, not the raw text, so details (especially numbers and reader comments) are second-hand and can be incomplete or truncated. Quotes are at most 15 words, taken from the fetch output.

Search was done with WebSearch (US-only index; chefkoch.de is blocked for this agent's user agent). Pages that only appeared as search snippets were **not** used as evidence; they are listed under failed pages where relevant.

Vocabulary trap found during the search: Swedish *kålrabbi* = kohlrabi (not swede); Norwegian *kålrabi* = swede; Swiss German *Bodenkohlrabi* = Steckrübe (swede). Swedish Diet Doctor "kålrabbigratäng" is therefore kohlrabi and was excluded.

### Synthesis — A
**Documented? Yes, clearly, but unevenly by region.**
- Common in **UK/Ireland** (swede-only "swede dauphinoise" G8/G9/G10, 1:1 swede–potato gratin by a known chef G11), **Germany/Switzerland** (Kartoffel-Steckrüben-Gratin G2, G6, G17; swede-only G5), and **North America** (rutabaga-only G1/G12, mixed G7/G22/G23). France: thin (diet-site editorial G16).
- **Nordic countries: weak.** No Finnish lanttu–peruna gratin found; Finland's swede tradition is the mashed lanttulaatikko. In Sweden, swede is a minor root (≈6% in a retail product, G25; unquantified in G4) inside potato-led rotfruktsgratäng; the big dairy-company recipes (G26, G27) leave it out. Norway: swede-only, pre-boiled (G3).

**Ratios used.** Mixed gratins range from 1:1 (G11) to potato-led about 3:2 or 6:5 (G2, G22); multi-root versions keep potato at 40–50% of the roots (G7, G17, G25). Swede-only gratins also exist and are not reported as failures, but they rely on cheese, béchamel or a long bake.

**Does the swede stay hard? This is the main risk, and the evidence agrees.**
- Two independent failure reports came from **raw, thin rutabaga with little or lean liquid**: G20 (3 mm, 45 min at 350°F, yogurt in place of cream) and G21 (≤⅛ inch, stock only, over an hour). Both stayed crunchy.
- Pre-cooking the swede is the usual fix, and is often applied to the swede only: blanch or boil 3–5 min (G1, G10, G11, G22), 10 min (G16, G18, G12 cubes), or simmer 10 min in the cream at 2 mm (G5, G9). G11 additionally cuts swede thicker (4 mm) than potato (3 mm) but pre-simmers it longer, so both finish together.
- Raw swede **can** work if slices are ~3 mm, the dish is **foil-covered**, and the total bake is long: G7 ≈ 90 min plus rest; G8 ≈ 90 min. Short raw bakes (G6 45 min, G2 40–50 min, G4 45 min) are untested on their pages.

**Does it hold and cut, or go watery?** The pages give little direct evidence; no reader reported a watery swede gratin on any page read. Indirect signals that the starch gap is real: G22 adds 3 tbsp flour because the roots release moisture; G7 adds 2 tsp flour and rests 25 min; the retail product G25 adds potato starch; swede-only recipes bind with lots of cheese (G1: 2 cups Gruyère), béchamel (G12), or crumbs (G8). Potato layers top and bottom (G7, G15) are a further hint that potato provides the structure. Egg is not used in any swede gratin found (but see B19, a broccoli-stem gratin bound with egg and soured cream).

**Flavour.** Opinions vary: mild, hard to taste and potato-like (G1 reader); slightly bitter, balanced by cream (G22); "spicy, pungent edge" (G12); slightly sweet with a hazelnut note (G16). Sharp accompaniments recur — Dijon (G7, G10), white wine (G7), apple or apple juice (G6, G18), anchovy or bacon (G8, G11), curry (G3) — which suggests cooks pair the swede with acid, sweetness or salt-umami rather than plain cream.

**Needless effort?** Pre-boiling the potatoes as well is not needed (G22 blanches swede only; G11 gives potato just 1 min). Simmering everything in cream (G5, G9) is a heavier but well-documented alternative to blanching.

**Conditions that matter (as documented):** slice thickness ≈2–3 mm; pre-cook the swede only (3–5 min blanch or 10 min in the cream), or bake covered for 90 min or more; use full cream rather than lean or acidic liquid; keep potato at least half of the roots, or add a starch (flour 2 tsp–3 tbsp) or plenty of cheese; rest before cutting (G7).

### Synthesis — B
**Documented? Yes, but narrowly and recently, and almost without quality reports.**
- **Stems as the main vegetable** in a fried patty or fritter: B1 (raw grated, stems only; B2 copies it), B4 (boiled then blended, stems only; B5 repeats the idea), B20 (raw grated stem + carrot), and the **stem + potato** latkes B21 (raw grated, squeezed) and B18 (steamed, with squeezed raw potato). These are zero-waste pieces from Australia, Brazil, the US and Ukraine, dated 2016–2026.
- **Not found** in Finnish, Swedish, Italian, French, German, Spanish or Korean as a stem-specific patty. There, the stem is either cooked with florets into whole-broccoli polpette, Puffer or galettes (B6–B8, B11, B16, B17) or used in salads, stir-fries, soups and pickles (B9, B13–B15, B22–B24).

**Preparation patterns.**
- **Peeling**: most sources peel off the fibrous skin (B7, B11, B13–B17, B21, B24). B1 relies on coarse grating to leave fibres behind; B19 and B22 say thin cutting makes peeling optional.
- **Raw vs cooked**: raw grated (B1, B20, B21) vs boiled or steamed first and blended (B4, B18, B11, B16). Japanese galettes use raw coarse-minced stem with starch and cheese (B6, B7).
- **Squeezing**: only B21 salts, drains and presses the raw grated stem (B18 squeezes only the potato). The stem flesh is described as water-rich (B9) and juicy (B22), so a squeeze step is plausible, but no source compares squeezed with unsqueezed.
- **Binders**: flour + water + cheese without egg (B1); egg + flour (B4, B20, B21); potato starch + cheese (B6–B8); chia/flax (B18). Potato or carrot is often added (B18, B20, B21) — the same partners as in Nordic porkkanapihvit.

**Does it work? Quality evidence is thin.** No failure report was found on any page read. Positive claims come from the publishers (B1 crisp, B4 crisp outside and soft inside, stems not noticeable; B20 soft inside) and two hands-on writer tests of whole-head galettes (B6 held its round shape thanks to cheese and starch; B7 crisp texture). Recipe-blog comments only show that processed stems work inside mixed broccoli fritters (B16).

**Flavour.** Mild, finer than the florets (B14), like asparagus (B15), lightly sweet and vegetal, sometimes cabbage-like (B13), sweetish (B9), nutty (B22). No source claims the stem is as sweet as carrot.

**Conditions and cautions.**
- Thin patties (1 tbsp batter, 1–2 min per side, B1) or pre-cooked stem (B4, B18) keep the stem from staying raw inside. Öko-Test advises against eating the stalk raw for sensitive stomachs (B15); grated stem in a short-fried thin fritter is cooked only briefly. This is an inference about the risk, not a documented problem.
- Stringy fibres: remove by peeling, or pick out after machine grating (B1).
- Fine chopping or grating matters for cohesion: large stem pieces make galettes crumble (B6).
- Needless effort: boiling then blending the stems (B4) gives a batter-like mass needing 6 tbsp flour; raw grating (B1, B21) is the shorter route, but no source compares the two.
