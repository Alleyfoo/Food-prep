# Evidence: falafel (raw-soaked pulse fritter) from dried peas (Pisum sativum)

Question: does anyone make falafel or a similar raw-soaked pulse fritter from dried peas (whole or split, yellow or green) instead of chickpeas or fava, and how does it turn out?

Date of research: 2026-11-24.
Read how: unless noted otherwise, pages were read with WebFetch. WebFetch returns a model-written summary of the page, not the raw text, so wording and small details may be off. Three PDFs were downloaded by WebFetch and their text was extracted locally (Read tool for P2 and P3; `pdftotext` for P15 and P17). That route reads the full text.
Quotes are 15 words or fewer. Everything else is paraphrased.

## Sources

### P1 — Sabrina Ghayour, "4-ingredient split pea falafels"
- URL: https://sabrinaghayour.com/4ingredient-split-pea-falafels
- Language: English. Site type: blog of a professional cookbook author (UK). No date shown. Read 2026-11-24 via WebFetch.
- Pulse: 200 g dried **yellow split** peas.
- Method: soaked 6 h in cold water, drained, used **raw**. Food processor, ground as fine as possible until it looks like fine couscous and is slightly pasty.
- Binder: **1 egg and 2 tbsp flour**. No baking powder. Garlic, dried parsley, salt and pepper; spices optional.
- Cooking: shallow-fried in about 1 inch of oil, a couple of minutes per side. The oil should make the mix "immediately sizzle"; no temperature given.
- Quality evidence: author's own claim of "Crunchy exterior and soft interior." She also says it is "never going to be better than the authentic falafel." Framed as a pantry fallback. She tried tinned chickpeas and says that does not work. No comments on the page.
- Note: the egg and flour depart from the no-binder norm. The page does not say why they are needed.

### P2 — Küchengötter (GU), "Falafel mit Hummus" (recipe 88097)
- URL: https://kuechengoetter.de/rezepte/download/Falafel-mit-Hummus-88097.pdf (print PDF of https://www.kuechengoetter.de/rezepte/falafel-mit-hummus-88097)
- Language: German. Site type: recipe site of the publisher Gräfe und Unzer (commercial publisher, edited recipes). Read 2026-11-24. WebFetch could not decode the PDF, so its saved copy was read in full with the Read tool.
- Pulse: 250 g **dried peas, green or yellow**. Whole or split is not stated. Dried fava or chickpeas are listed as substitutes, so peas are the primary pulse.
- Method: soaked overnight, used **raw**. Ground with onion, garlic, parsley and coriander in a food processor as finely as possible. Spices, 2 tsp salt and **1 tsp Natron (baking soda)** mixed in.
- Binder: **none**.
- Cooking: deep-fried 4–5 min until golden. Oil is ready when bubbles form at once around a wooden spoon handle.
- Quality evidence: one handling note. The balls cannot be rolled; you press the mass into shape between your hands ("Rollen kann man sie nicht"). So the paste is soft and loose but still shapeable. No ratings in the PDF.

### P3 — Küchengötter (GU), "Erbsen-Minz-Falafel – vegetarisch" (recipe 94411)
- URL: https://www.kuechengoetter.de/rezepte/download/Erbsen-Minz-Falafel-vegetarisch-94411.pdf (print PDF of https://www.kuechengoetter.de/rezepte/erbsen-minz-falafel-vegetarisch-94411)
- Language: German. Site type: publisher recipe site. Read 2026-11-24. Saved PDF read in full with the Read tool.
- Pulse: 250 g **dried green split peas** ("getrocknete grüne Splittererbsen") plus 100 g frozen peas, scalded.
- Method: soaked **at least 12 h**, rinsed and drained, used **raw**. Put through the **fine disc of a meat grinder** (Fleischwolf) or puréed.
- Binder: 1 tbsp flour, 1 tsp baking powder. Also onion, garlic, mint, cumin and salt. Kneaded smooth and shaped with wet hands into about 20 golf-ball-sized balls.

- Quality evidence: none (no ratings in the PDF). Note the meat-grinder option: that is the traditional falafel grinding route.

### P4 — Hodmedod's, "Green Pea Falafel" (Julius Fiedler / Baking Hermann), 4 May 2023
- URL: https://www.hodmedods.co.uk/blogs/recipes/green-pea-falafel
- Language: English. Site type: blog of a **commercial seller** of British-grown pulses (it sells the peas used). Read 2026-11-24 via WebFetch.
- Pulse: 200 g British-grown **green split peas**.
- Method: soaked overnight in 3 times their volume of water, used **raw**. Food processor, pulsed to a **coarse** pea "flour". Mixed with onion, garlic, green chilli, coriander, parsley, cumin, ground coriander and salt. Chilled at least 15 min, then shaped into 16 balls of 30 g.
- Binder: **none**. No baking powder.
- Cooking: oil at about **170 °C**. Test one ball, which should be golden within 2 min, then fry 4–6 at a time for 4 min.
- Quality evidence: two stated pitfalls. (1) Do not grind too finely, or the falafel turn out dense ("would make the falafel rather dense"). (2) Overcrowding the pan means the balls "might fall apart." The result is described as crispy and dark golden. No comments.

### P15 — Janhager, J. (2020). *Suitability of different pulses in falafel making – a new application for Swedish foods.* MSc thesis, SLU Uppsala, Molecular Sciences 2020:05. Co-supervised by Sevan Hummus Factory AB.
- URL: https://stud.epsilon.slu.se/15789/7/janhager_j_200706.pdf
- Language: English (Swedish summary). Site type: **academic thesis**, with instrumental texture analysis (TPA) and a consumer acceptance test. Read 2026-11-24: WebFetch could not decode the PDF, so the saved copy's full text was extracted with `pdftotext` and read directly. Note: a search snippet called this a "2007" thesis. That is wrong; it is 2020.
- Pulses screened: black bean, borlotti bean, **Bruno field pea (Pisum sativum var. arvense)**, chickpea (reference), Gloria faba bean, Gotland lentil, three lupins, and **yellow pea (Pisum sativum)**. Whole or split is **not stated**.
- Method (same for all): rinsed, soaked in cold water 1:3 w/v for **about 12 h**, used **raw**. Food processor at medium speed until a mouldable batter forms. Simplified recipe of **pulse, NaHCO₃ and salt only** (no onion or herbs), rested 10 min. Shaped with a 40 mm falafel tool, deep-fried **2 min at 180 °C** in canola oil.
- Structure: every pulse, yellow pea included, **formed and fried as a binder-free falafel**. Only the lupins were noted as soft and falling apart easily (low cohesiveness).
- Physical results for yellow pea:
  - **Lowest batter moisture content** of all samples (significant).
  - **Significantly the highest hardness** of all falafels.
  - Among the lowest water loss during frying.
  - Lightest interior, together with chickpea.
  - Water uptake during soaking about 72 % of dry weight, against about 97.5 % for chickpea. This figure is read from a table whose layout was garbled in extraction, so treat it as approximate. The text does confirm the lowest batter moisture.
- Screening exclusion: the second Pisum sample, **Bruno field pea**, was dropped before sensory testing "due to high dryness". Lupins were dropped for bitterness and faba for unpleasant flavour.
- Consumer test (n = 54, 9-point hedonic scale, means from Table 4 as extracted):

  | Pulse | Appearance | Texture | Flavour | Overall |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Yellow pea | 6.6 | **4.6** (lowest) | 5.3 | **5.4** (lowest) |
  | Chickpea (reference) | 6.0 | 5.8 | 6.2 | 6.0 |
  | Gotland lentil (best overall) | 6.2 | 6.0 | 6.7 | 6.6 |

  The only significant overall-liking difference was yellow pea against lentil; yellow pea against chickpea was not significant.
- Comments from panellists: all falafels were called dry, but yellow pea had "especially many comments regarding them being too dry". Its appearance comments were positive.
- Author's conclusion: all tested pulses "apart from yellow peas" could readily replace chickpeas. Texture liking correlated with high water uptake in soaking, high batter moisture and low hardness. In a follow-up optimisation on lentils, soaking time (10–13 h best) mattered and NaHCO₃ in the soak water had little effect.
- Caveats: one variety, one soak time, a bare recipe without onion or herbs (which add moisture), 2-minute fry, a small panel. The thesis states standard deviations were large.

## Synthesis

### 1. Is the transfer documented?
**Yes, but almost entirely as a modern substitution, not a tradition.**
- **Western home recipes** for falafel from **raw-soaked dried split peas**, all read: English (P1, P4, P5, P6; P8 is SEO), German publisher recipes (P2, P3), Spanish (P9). Green and yellow split peas both appear. Of the readable falafel sources, only P2 and P9 say "dried peas" without naming the form, and P2 allows green or yellow.
- **Commercial products**: Sweden has a yellow-pea falafel (82 % peas, bicarbonate, no binder listed; P16). The USA has a New England yellow-pea "field falafel" in hospital food service (P17).
- **Academic test**: one Swedish MSc thesis (P15) made binder-free falafel from yellow pea and field pea alongside eight other pulses.
- **Traditional relatives**: South Indian and Maharashtrian *pattani/vatana masala vada* (P10, P11, P12, P13) use **whole dried yellow, white or green peas**, soaked overnight, ground raw and coarse, and deep-fried. Binder is none or a spoon of besan or urad dal. This is the closest structural equivalent with a culinary history, though the evidence is a handful of blog and user recipes. Caribbean **phulourie** (P14) uses raw-ground soaked yellow split peas, but as a **flour-and-baking-powder drop batter**, a different structure.
- **Not found**: any Finnish home recipe or Finnish pea-based falafel product (Fallero is faba, P21); Polish, Dutch, Danish, Arabic or Chinese raw-dried-pea falafel. Wikipedia's split-pea article lists no fritter use (P22).

### 2. Does it hold together and set in hot oil without egg or flour?
**Per the evidence, yes, it can.**
- Binder-free raw pea pastes are documented as frying successfully: P2, P4, P6, P11, P12, the SLU lab falafels P15, and the P16 ingredient list.
- About half the home recipes still add a little binder: 1 tbsp flour (P3, P8), 1 tbsp besan (P10), 2–3 tbsp rice flour (P5), 70 g breadcrumbs (P9), egg plus flour (P1). None says the pea paste failed without it. These read as habit or insurance, not as documented necessity.
- Handling reports: the paste is soft and sticky, so you press it into shape rather than roll it (P2, P6). Overcrowding the fryer can break the balls up (P4).

### 3. How does it turn out? (quality evidence)
- **The only controlled evidence is unfavourable for yellow peas** (P15).
  - In the same bare recipe, yellow-pea falafel had the **lowest water uptake and batter moisture** and the **significantly highest hardness**.
  - Consumers rated its texture lowest (4.6/9) and overall liking lowest (5.4 against 6.0 for chickpea and 6.6 for lentil). It was significantly worse than lentil but **not significantly worse than chickpea**.
  - Panellists' most frequent complaint was **"too dry"**.
  - The other Pisum sample (field pea) was excluded for high dryness.
  - The thesis concluded every tested pulse except yellow peas could readily replace chickpeas.
  - Caveat: bare recipe (pulse, salt and bicarbonate only), 12 h soak, 2-min fry. Onion and herbs, which every real recipe uses, would add moisture.
- **Anecdotal evidence is mildly positive** but weak:
  - Authors say "easier than chickpeas, which never soften" (P5) and "lighter and crisper" than dal vada (P12).
  - Ghayour (P1) calls hers crunchy outside and soft inside, but "never" as good as real falafel.
  - Ratings exist only for variant methods: P7 is par-cooked and baked (4.91/22, one bland review); P13's method is unknown (4.3/27). No home cook's comment on a raw-paste pea falafel reporting a real outcome was found.
- **No source reports a "pea-soup" taste.** P7 deliberately leans into pea-soup seasoning. No source reports a raw or beany taste.

### 4. Conditions that matter, per sources
- **Grind**: coarse, not fine. Hodmedod's says a fine grind gives dense falafel (P4). The vada recipes grind coarsely with minimal water (P10, P11). Ghayour grinds fine and then needs egg and flour (P1); that may be consistent, but it is not proven. A meat grinder's fine disc is an option (P3).
- **Soak**: overnight (8–12 h+) in plenty of water (3× volume, P4; 1:3 w/v, P15). Split peas also appear with 6 h (P1). For lentils, P15 found soak time drives water uptake and softness (10–13 h best) and bicarbonate in the soak adds little. Since yellow peas took up the least water in P15, a **longer soak** is the obvious lever, but no source tested it for peas.
- **Moisture**: liking of texture tracked batter moisture and low hardness (P15). The recipes that work add onion, herbs, grated potato or carrot (P11), or frozen peas (P3). Keep water low when grinding (P11). Drain well.
- **Leavening**: baking soda or baking powder in the mix (P2, P3, P5, P15, and the P16 product) or in the soak (P6, P8).
- **Frying**: 170–180 °C (P4, P15); fry in small batches (P4). Times are 2 min (lab, 40 mm balls), 4 min (P4) or 4–5 min (P2).
- **Whole vs split**: **no source compares them directly.** Western recipes use split peas. Indian vadas use whole dried peas, and P11 skins them after soaking. Whole Finnish *herneet* would carry seed coats. P15 blamed undispersed seed-coat pieces for some measurement variance, but that is a general remark. Split (already dehulled) is the lower-risk default; whole peas probably need skinning or accepting a coarser, flecked texture (inference, not sourced).

### 5. Safety (raw soaked peas)
- No source says raw-soaked peas in falafel are unsafe or unpleasant.
- BfR says peas are **low in lectins** (P18). BfR and EFSA (P18, P19) still give generic advice to boil soaked dried pulses, and neither addresses frying. EFSA calls roasting "less effective".
- Traditional falafel and vada likewise fry raw-soaked chickpea, fava or pea paste, so the practice matches established cuisine. Fry small pieces all the way through.

### 6. Any evidence it is poor or needlessly complicated?
- **Poor**: the one systematic test (P15) ranked yellow-pea falafel least liked, mainly for dry, hard texture. Ghayour (P1) calls hers a second-best fallback.
- **Complicated**: no source calls it harder than chickpea falafel; one says it is easier (P5). Effort is the same overnight soak, grind and fry. The binders in some recipes add a step that the binder-free sources suggest is optional.
- **Overall**: the transfer exists and holds together. Expect a firmer, drier crumb than chickpea, and compensate with a longer soak, a coarse grind, moist aromatics and leavening. Field evidence is thin: no tested outcome reports for raw-paste pea falafel beyond authors' own claims and one lab study.
