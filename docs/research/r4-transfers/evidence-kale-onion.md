## Part A: Kale and close relatives as a wrapper

### K1. Yemek.com: Karalahana Sarması (Black Sea)
- URL: https://yemek.com/tarif/karalahana-sarmasi/
- Language: Turkish. Site type: large recipe portal, ed + UGC comments.
- Read: WebFetch summary.
- Documents: karalahana (non-heading kale, *B. oleracea* var. *acephala*), 3 bunches. Leaves blanched in batches for **3–4 min**. Thick hard veins are cut out, and leftover tough parts are flattened with the flat of a knife. Whole leaves are not cut. Filling is minced meat, rice, grated onion, tomato paste, mint and thyme. Rolls are packed tightly, weighted with an inverted plate, and simmered on low with butter and tomato paste. The FAQ says fresh thin leaves can be rolled without blanching. Rock salt in the blanching water is said to stop the leaves from over-softening. Uncooked rolls can be frozen.
- Quality evidence: 4.6/5 from 7 ratings and 3 reviews. A 2025 comment says the people who eat it like it a lot. A 2022 comment suggests lining the pot with the leftover stems. No failures reported. The page has a "published 3 Nov 2026" date, but its comments go back to 2022, so the recipe is older than that date.

### K4. Kure Ansiklopedi: Karalahana Sarması
- URL: https://kureansiklopedi.com/tr/detay/karalahana-sarmasi-d88f8
- Language: Turkish. Site type: online encyclopedia, ed.
- Read: WebFetch summary.
- Documents: a traditional dish of the Eastern Black Sea (Rize, Trabzon, Artvin). It says Rize and Düzce versions have geographical-indication registration. Karalahana is named as *B. oleracea* var. *acephala*. Leaves are boiled until they lose their firmness. Fillings are rice, fine bulgur or cracked corn (mısır yarması) with mince, and the dish was a winter staple, sometimes made communally (imece).
- Quality evidence: establishes it as a long, widespread tradition with GI status. No outcome data.

### K5. Yemek.com: "Sarma çeşitleri püf noktaları" (sarma tips)

- Language: Turkish. Site type: portal editorial (Cemre Özgüzel, 12 Apr 2017).

### K6. Küchengötter (GU): Gefüllte Schwarzkohlröllchen – türkische Meze
- URL: https://www.kuechengoetter.de/rezepte/download/Gefullte-Schwarzkohlrollchen-turkische-Meze-86241.pdf
- Canonical page named in the PDF: https://www.kuechengoetter.de/rezepte/gefuellte-schwarzkohlroellchen-tuerkische-meze-86241 (not fetched separately).
- Language: German. Site type: publisher (Gräfe und Unzer) recipe site, ed.
- Read: WebFetch could not decode the PDF, so I read it in full through the saved PDF.
- Documents: 600 g **Schwarzkohl (Karalahana)**, with kohlrabi, white cabbage or savoy leaves allowed as substitutes. Leaves blanched in salted water in batches for **5 min**, shocked, then **halved lengthwise with the midribs removed**. About ½ tbsp filling goes on each half-leaf, so the rolls are small. Filling is lamb or beef mince, Turkish short-grain rice, coarse bulgur, tomato, parsley and pomegranate vinegar. Rolls are weighted with a plate and simmered 30–40 min. Rated "leicht" (easy), with 60–90 min prep.
- Quality evidence: publisher difficulty rating only. It is useful because it shows the rib can be removed by splitting the leaf into two narrow halves.

### K12. Kitchen Dreaming: Stuffed Kale Rolls (oven-baked, ground beef)
- URL: https://kitchendreaming.com/stuffed-kale-rolls
- Language: English (US). Site type: blog (Ronda Eagle).
- Read: WebFetch summary.
- Documents: 7 oz kale, variety **not named**. Thick vein removed. Each leaf dipped in boiling salted water for only **a few seconds** until bright green. About ¼ cup of raw mince and raw rice per leaf. Baked covered at 350°F for **about 90 min**. Advice: if the kale is tough, blanch longer. If rolls fall apart, use larger leaves and roll tightly. If leaves are too small to tuck the sides, leave the ends open.
- Quality evidence: 5★ from 2 votes. A 2023 commenter used garden **Red Russian kale** (a flat, oak-leaf type) in a slow cooker for hours, found the kale very tender, and preferred it to cabbage rolls. A 2018 commenter said the kale was hardly noticeable under the sauce. A 2016 commenter called it a new favourite. This is the strongest first-hand "kale instead of cabbage worked" evidence found, but the variety in it is not curly.

## Part B: Onion as a vessel

### O1. Giallozafferano blog "Allacciate il grembiule": Cipolle ripiene (alla ligure)
- URL: https://blog.giallozafferano.it/allacciateilgrembiule/ricetta-cipolle-ripiene/
- Language: Italian. Site type: blog hosted on the Giallozafferano platform. Images uploaded around May/June 2025.
- Read: WebFetch summary.
- Documents: **onions halved and the large outer layers separated**, set cut side up "come fossero delle ciotoline" (like little bowls). **No blanching**, which the author says is unnecessary. The small or broken onion pieces are blended with marjoram and mortadella or ham into the filling (**recombination**). The filling adds boiled mashed potato, Parmesan and 1–2 eggs, with a breadcrumb top. Baked in a fan oven at 200°C for **about 20 min**.
- Quality evidence: 4.2/5 (count not shown), no comments. The author calls it very easy and her favourite of the stuffed vegetables. Rated easy and economical.

### O2. Giallozafferano blog "Renata Briano": Cipolle ripiene di tonno alla ligure
- URL: https://blog.giallozafferano.it/renatabriano/ricetta-cipolle-ripiene-tonno-alla-ligure/
- Language: Italian. Site type: blog (published 22 Jul 2024, updated 17 Nov 2026).
- Read: WebFetch summary.
- Documents: Tropea or white onions **blanched 5 min**, **halved, and the concave layers separated into little boats**. Small inner layers are chopped into the filling (**recombination**). Filling is milk-soaked bread, tuna, marjoram, 3 eggs and Parmesan. Baked in a static oven at 180°C for about 30 min, served warm or at room temperature. Presented as an old Ligurian recipe.

### O3. Giallozafferano blog "A tavola con Tea": Verdure ripiene alla ligure

### O4. Giallozafferano blog "Folletto panettiere": Cipolle ripiene, ricetta tipica piemontese

### O5. Ville de Nice: Les Farcis Niçois, Les Oignons (Li Céba)
- URL: https://www.nice.fr/ressources/les-farcis-nicois (serves a PDF)
- Language: French (with Niçard names). Site type: **city government (inst)**, "Cuisine Nissarde" label. Undated.
- Read: WebFetch could not decode it, so I **read the full text through the saved PDF**.
- Documents: 6 large white onions for 8 people. Onions are **peeled, halved across the equator, and blanched 10 min in salted boiling water**. The centre is scooped out and the outer layers kept "deux par deux" (two layers thick) as shells. **The rest of the onion is chopped finely into the farce** (**recombination**) with petit salé, dry mountain cheese (Parmesan or sbrinz), egg, milk-soaked bread, basil, garlic and parsley. Breadcrumbs and oil go on top. Baked at **180°C for 30 min**. The courgette sheet in the same PDF gives the matching procedure: blanch 15 min, halve, scoop, recombine, 180°C for 30 min. A general note says one common farce can be used for all the farcis, mixed with the flesh of every vegetable except tomatoes and peppers.
- Quality evidence: this is a municipal standard recipe, so it is authoritative for the tradition. It has no outcome data. It shows that onion and courgette are treated as **equivalent vessels in the same procedure**.

## Synthesis

### Question A: kale leaves as the wrapper
**Is it documented?** Yes, widely, but almost entirely with **flat or large-leaved *acephala* types**, not curly kale.
- **Turkish karalahana sarması** is an established regional tradition. It has GI-registered versions (K4), many portal recipes (K1–K3, K5), and appears in German publisher form (K6).
- **Brazilian charuto/charutinho de couve**, made with couve, typically the collard-type couve-manteiga, is common home cooking on UGC sites (K7–K10). Users rate it as practical and quick.
- **US collard rolls** (K11) and **lacinato/cavolo nero rolls** (K16–K19) are a recurring modern substitution for cabbage rolls.
- Unnamed kale in North American home recipes (K12–K14, K21, K23, K24) shows "kale instead of cabbage" is a known swap. The one commenter who named a variety used Red Russian, a flat type (K12).
- I found **no Finnish, Swedish, Dutch or German (Grünkohl) kale-wrapper tradition**, and **no European-Portuguese couve-galega roll recipe**.

**Does curly kale work?** There is **no direct evidence either way**. No page I could read explicitly uses curly *sabellica* leaves as single wrappers and reports the result. The one page titled for curly kale rolls (metro.ca) returned 403. The indirect evidence points away from curly kale:
- One recipe explicitly asks for "non-curly kale" (K17).
- A test-kitchen recipe chose lacinato and described curly kale as firmer with tougher stems (K16).
- Every named-variety recipe uses a flat type (karalahana, couve, collard, lacinato, Red Russian).

Home-cook workarounds would fit curly kale's small, frilly leaves: stacking several small leaves per roll (K13), "double-leafing" over tears (K14), cutting leaves into rectangles and scattering the scraps (K24), and leaving the ends open when leaves are too small to tuck (K12). This is inference from adjacent evidence, not a tested curly-kale result.

**What conditions matter?**
- *Rib:* nearly every source removes it. The methods are a V-cut at the base keeping the leaf whole (K11, K15), cutting along both sides of the rib (K16), splitting the leaf in half lengthwise (K6, K11), or flattening tough remnants with a knife (K1).
- *Blanching:* times range from a few seconds to 5 min. Raw rolling is possible for lacinato and cavolo nero (K17, K19) and for fresh, thin karalahana (K1). The typical ranges are lacinato and collard 30 s–1 min (K16, K22), karalahana 1–4 min (K1, K3, K5), Schwarzkohl 5 min (K6), and collard 4 min (K11). Tough leaves get longer blanching or steaming of 7 min or more (K12, K13).
- *Leaf size:* the wrapper must be large, about 5–6 in across for lacinato (K16), and recipes advise "larger leaves" against falling apart (K12). Large karalahana and collard leaves are cut down or halved (K2, K9, K11).
- *Cooking:* long wet cooking softens the leaf reliably. Examples are 90 min covered in the oven (K12), a slow cooker for hours with "very tender" leaves (K12 comment), and 40–50 min covered braising with no blanch at all (K23).
- *Bitterness:* not reported as a problem anywhere. One commenter said the kale was barely noticeable under the sauce (K12).

**Evidence that it is poor or laborious?** None found. Several sources say the opposite: kale or karalahana is **easier than cabbage** because the leaves need no coring and peeling off a head (K5, K15, K18). The only failure signals are generic: leaves tear (K14) or rolls fall apart if the leaves are small (K12).

### Question B: hollowed onion as the vessel
**Is it documented?** Yes, very widely and across many traditions. There are two distinct constructions.
1. **Cup or shell (closest to zucchine ripiene):**
   - Ligurian cipolle ripiene (O1–O3) and Niçois oignons farcis (O5) halve the onion and keep the outer layers as shallow boats. Liguria blanches 0–10 min and Nice blanches 10 min.
   - Piedmontese (O4), German (O8), Swedish (O9) and North American (O11) versions hollow the whole bulb to 2–3 layers, with a lid cut off. They pre-boil the whole onion about 20 min (O4, O8), boil until almost soft (O9), or skip boiling (O11).
   - Asturian cebollas rellenas (O6, O7) hollow raw whole onions and compensate with 2.5–3 h braising.
   - In the Ligurian (O3) and Niçois (O5) versions, the onion is treated as an **exact procedural twin of the courgette**: same blanch-scoop-recombine-bake steps, same farce, 180°C for 30–40 min.
2. **Layer rolls (dolma style):** Turkish soğan dolması (O18–O20), Greek sougania of Lesvos (O13, O14), Levantine and Egyptian mahshi basal (O15–O17), and Ottolenghi (O21). One slit is cut to the centre, the onion boiled 5–15 min, then the layers are peeled off and rolled around a rice and meat filling, then braised or baked 30–60 min.

**Recombination (scooped onion back into the filling):** this is the norm in the cup tradition. It appears in Liguria (O1, O2, O3, where the onion pulp is pooled with the courgette pulp), Nice (O5, explicitly), Piedmont (O4), Sweden (O9), ATK (O10), The Endless Meal (O11, partly) and Greek sougania (O13, O14). The alternatives are a bed under the onions (O6, O16), the sauce (O7), or a token amount in the filling with the rest scattered around (O8).

**Quality and conditions:**
- Outcomes are mostly positive: 4.96/5 from 25 ratings for whole hollowed cups (O11), 4/5 from 16 at ATK (O10), and strongly positive first-hand reports (O13, O21). Italian blog star ratings run 4.1–4.6 with small or unknown counts.
- The main failure mode is **layers tearing or falling apart when overboiled** (O12, O15). A snug pot prevents onions moving and coming apart (O7).
- **Handling is awkward:** ATK abandoned the "bowl" concept because cooked onions are slippery and went to ring shells (O10).
- **Effort is the main complaint:** "a little time consuming" (O16), "un poquito laboriosa" (O7), and two 2★ reviews calling it cumbersome or not worth the effort (O12). Others call it easy (O1, O4, O11, O13, O15, O17). GU rates its hollowed onions medium difficulty against easy for karalahana rolls (O8 vs K6).
- **No source in the set explicitly says the onion becomes sweet.** One caramelised result is reported (O15), and the Piedmontese sweet amaretti variant is mentioned (O4).

**Answer:** the onion-for-courgette swap is well established, and in Nice and Liguria it is documented as the same procedure. Settings that hold across sources:
- Halve across the equator.
- Blanch about 5–10 min, or boil about 20 min if keeping the bulb whole.
- Keep 2–3 outer layers as the shell.
- Chop the rest of the onion into the farce.
- Bake at 180–200°C for 20–40 min.

The evidence that it is needlessly laborious is limited to a minority of effort complaints, and those are mostly about the layer-roll variant.
