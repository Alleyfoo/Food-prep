# R2 step 2: observed mechanisms

For each preparation in `recipes.md`: what gives it structure, which physical
change makes it hold, whether a separate binder is needed, how water is
handled, and which steps the recipes agree are essential. The mechanisms in
section 2 were named **after** reading the recipes, as short descriptions of
what the recipes do. They are not a classification chosen beforehand. Recipe
ids (E5a, M12c, A18a) point into `recipes.md`; RecipeNLG percentages come
from `recipenlg-counts.md`.

Physical names (gelatinisation, retrogradation, coagulation, osmosis) are
general food science (`mcgee`). They are used to name what a recipe does.
Where a recipe states its own reason, the reason is quoted or paraphrased and
marked "stated". Where only the science explains a step, the text says so.

## 1. Per preparation

"Own phase" means the main material itself supplies something cohesive once
it has been transformed. "Binder needed?" asks whether recipes of the dish
*must* add a separate material for cohesion. A binder that some recipes add
and others leave out is not needed.

## 2. Mechanisms, as the recipes show them

Each mechanism is a physical change that the recipes use to make a structure
hold. A dish often combines several. Dishes that **look alike** can hold by
**different mechanisms**: rösti (H1/H2), latkes (H1 + H5), raggmunk (H6b)
and courgette fritters (H6a + H5) are all flat fried cakes of grated
vegetable.

**H1. Starch released from a raw tuber, gelatinised by the pan.** Grating or
blending breaks the cells; the starch either stays on the strands (E7a: "do
not rinse") or is recovered from the expressed liquid and returned (E2b, E3a,
E4a-b, E5a-c, E10, A10a). Reasons stated: the starch "thickens and sets on
heating" (E4a), "helps the strands stick together" (E7a), makes flour
unnecessary (E5b, E5c), is "the key point" (A10a). Bought potato starch does
the same job (E3b, A10b, E9a).

**H2. Starch already gelatinised, firmed by cooling.** Boiled potato, cooked
rice, porridge, soaked bread and roux-milk gel are cohesive by themselves once
cool: boiled rösti (E1a, E1c), aloo tikki, korokke, pierogi filling,
arancini, croquetas, karjalanpiirakka porridge, porkkanalaatikko E17a. The
recipes repeatedly **cool** before shaping (section 4, convergence 9).
Science: retrogradation; the recipes say only "easier to shape" (M11a),
"easier to cut" (M15b).

**H3. Raw starch partly gelatinised by scalding.** Boiling liquid poured over
a raw starch to make a workable dough: Thüringer Klöße (boiling water or hot
mash over the raw mass, E10a, E10c) and pão de queijo (boiling milk and oil
over cassava starch, A16a-b). Two independent traditions; neither explains it
beyond "to scald" (A16a, inference). A cooked starch mixed into a raw one
(E8, E9, E10) is a milder form of the same idea: E9a states that boiled
potato helps the mass "bind and not go mushy".

**H4. Raw soaked pulses ground to a paste.** Soaking hydrates the seed;
grinding without cooking gives a paste that sets when fried or steamed
(science: the starch gelatinises and the proteins set; the recipes do not
explain it). No binder in ta'ameya/falafel (M1, M2), medu vada (A3), akara
(A18) or moi moi (A19). The control the recipes name is **water**: little in
the grind, short soaks (A3a, A18a-b, A19a); and **grind fineness** (M1c).
Cooked pulses do not behave this way: the native recipes never cook them,
while the American corpus, which cooks or cans them (38% canned), adds flour
(43%), crumbs (30%) or egg (28%) to falafel.

**H5. Egg protein coagulating.** In four positions:
- **H5a, as the continuous phase**: tortilla, frittata, chawanmushi,
  quiche, egg custard bakes (E17b-c, E14a).
- **H5b, as a minority addition to a vegetable mass**: courgette fritters,
  latkes, patties.
- **H5c, as glue between a piece and its dry coating** (egg-crumb:
  arancini, croquetas, korokke).
- **H5d, as an aerated foam coat**: capeado, A14.

In H5b the recipes treat egg as **adjustable or optional**. The Belarusian
draniki classic has none (E5b). E17a and E14a make it optional. Ingmar ties
the amount to the equipment (E18b). The Vietnamese nem recipes cap it as a
**source of water** (A13b-c), and A18a rejected it.

**H6. Flour hydrated by water already present, set by heat.**
- **H6a, a small dry phase in a vegetable-majority mass.** The flour, and
  egg (H5b), take up the water left after removal: mücver, beignets M18a,
  placki z cukinii. The flour's stated job is "to absorb the remaining
  liquid" (M4a). The mass is often rested so the flour hydrates ("svälla",
  E18c; 15 min, M3b; let the batter swell, E19a; "durchziehen", E11b).
- **H6a', the vegetable's own salt-drawn juice is the only liquid**: onion
  pakoda (A1a) and shredded hobak-jeon (A9b), both "do not add water".
- **H6b, a liquid batter as the matrix** with the vegetable as inclusion:
  raggmunk (E6), Corsican beignets (M18b), okonomiyaki (A7, with grated yam),
  kakiage (A5), corn fritters (RecipeNLG 92% flour, 2% water removal).

**H7. Containment.** A shell, leaf, dough or husk holds a filling that need
not cohere by itself: the Finnish cabbage-roll filling is "loose" (E12b).
Stuffed vegetables, rolls, dumplings, pies, tamales. The wrapper has its own
requirements (H7 in `requirements.md`): it must bend without tearing (leaves
boiled, RecipeNLG 76%; husks soaked, A15) or stand up as a vessel. The
filling must not release water against it (A11a: "very crucial", or the
water splits the wrapper). A **temporary** container also counts: nori or
baking paper under kakiage until the batter sets (A5a); a mould for moi moi
(A19b).

**H8. Packing, weight and rest.** Rolls packed tight in a leaf-lined pot
(E13a: "so they do not unroll"), a plate on top (M6a), light pressing and a
covered rest (M6b). The structure is held from outside until the rice has
swollen and set.

**H9. A crust as a structural shell.** Frying or baking forms a dry surface
that holds the piece before the inside has set. Eight sources say not to
turn or touch until the crust has formed, or the cake breaks: E4a, E5a, E7a,
E19a, M3b, A4a, A6a, A10a. A cold pan or cold oil gives fat uptake and breaking
(E7a, E19a, M3b, A13b, M13b). A lid traps steam that softens the crust
(E5c). A second fry re-crisps a crust softened by a wet inside (A12b, A13b-c,
A3b). In korokke the inside fails the crust: steam from a wet filling bursts
it (A6a).

**H10. A thickened liquid in a layered dish.** Layers held by a phase that
thickens around them: potato starch in milk and cream (gratin, M15b, stated),
reduced sauce and melted cheese (parmigiana), an egg in the sauce "so it
holds when cut" (M9b), egg-milk or reduced cream in kaalilaatikko (E14).
Chilling before cutting recurs (M15b).

**H11. Air held in a paste.** Not a way of holding together, but a recurring
structural step inside H4, H5, H6b and tamale masa: akara whipped until it
doubles (A18a), medu vada beaten (A3a), ta'ameya beaten (M1c), fat beaten
for masa and proved by a **float test** (A15a-b), egg-white foam (A14),
okonomiyaki folded and not pressed (A7b), egg beaten frothy (M12b, M20a).
Several add the rest of the ingredients **last, by folding**, to keep the air
(A18a, A14a).

Seen but outside vegetables: meat fillings stirred one way until sticky
(A11a, A12b), stated to make the filling hold together.

## 3. How water is handled

Every pattern manages the total free water. The recipes show nine ways to do
it, and a dish usually allows more than one:

| | Way | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| W1 | Salt, rest, then squeeze or drain | courgette E19b, M4a, M18a, A11a-b; potato E5a; spinach M8a; aubergine M9 (RecipeNLG: 9% of courgette fritters write salt-and-rest) |
| W2 | Squeeze or press without salting | mücver M3a-b, bolinho A17a, rårakor E7, latkes, kroppkakor E8 (RecipeNLG squeeze 28-31%) |
| W3 | Drain by gravity or weight | Reibekuchen sieve (E2b), polpette under a weight 30 min (M10a), eggplant layers weighted (M9b) |
| W4 | Settle the expressed liquid, return its starch | E2b, E3a, E4, E5, E10, A10a; RecipeNLG 3% of latkes |
| W5 | Drive the water off with heat before combining | tortilla oil step "to dehydrate" (M12c); frittata sauté; filling cooked "until its liquid evaporates" (M5a); korokke meat dried, potato boiled in skin (A6a); roast rather than boil (M10b); boiled potato stored two days (E1c) |
| W6 | Absorb it with a dry addition, and let it swell | flour until not runny (A17b); fried vermicelli "absorbs moisture" (A11b); taro, potato, crumbs (A13b); poha or rice flour (A2a); crumbs per layer (M9a); bought starch (E3b) |
| W7 | Prevent its release | salt late (E5c, E7a, A1b, A3a, A11a final salt); grate just before frying (E5b, E7a); cabbage added last (A7a); slices kept out of water (M15b) |
| W8 | Use it | as the batter's only liquid (A1a, A9b); as the cooking liquid of raw rice inside the vegetable (M7a); reduce other liquid instead (M8b); keep some cooking liquid in the bake (E14b) |
| W9 | Dry the surface before coating | pat dry (A9a, A14a twice, A4a); flour dust "absorbs the moisture so the egg sticks" (A14a) |

**Salt timing follows the water's fate.** The recipes seem to contradict each
other on salt: early (E5a, E19b, A11a, A9b) or late (E5c, E7a, A1b, A3a).
All of them give the same reason, that salt draws water. The rule that
reconciles them is in the recipes themselves. **Salt early when a removal or
use step follows** (squeeze E5a; drain E19b; make it the batter liquid A9b).
**Salt late when nothing will remove the water** (E5c fries at once; A1b's
vegetables are already drained; A11a salts a second time just before
wrapping). No recipe read salts early and then leaves the water in.

| Flour dust before batter | makes the batter cling *less*, a thin coat (A4a) | makes it stick *more*, or the kakiage falls apart (A5a) | A4a is about coat thickness on a single piece, A5a about gluing pieces together |
| Lid on | yes: frittata (M20a), okonomiyaki (A7b-c) | no: deruny (E5c), okonomiyaki (A7a) | a lid steams the top, which sets an egg top but softens a crust |
| Egg in a cabbage-roll filling | yes (E13a; RecipeNLG 49%) | no (E12a-b) | containment makes it optional |

## 6. Is a separate binder needed?

The evidence answers this per material, not per dish shape.

- **No**, when the main material can be transformed into its own cohesive
  phase:
  - raw starch released (H1);
  - starch cooked and cooled (H2);
  - raw soaked pulses ground (H4);
  - a filling that is contained (H7).

  In these dishes the recipes that add egg or flour are a variant, and the
  same dish exists without them (E5b-c, E1, M1-M2, E12).
- **Yes**, when the main material is watery and starch-poor and is used raw
  or barely cooked: courgette, carrot, spinach, aubergine pulp. Every web
  recipe of courgette fritters and vegetable patties adds egg and/or flour or
  crumbs. RecipeNLG: courgette fritters 92% egg, other vegetable fritters 81%.
  The one exception uses the vegetable's own juice to hydrate flour (A9b),
  which is still an added flour.
- **The same dish can need one in one tradition and not in another**,
  because the material was transformed differently: falafel from raw soaked
  chickpeas (no binder) vs canned chickpeas (flour, crumbs, egg in the
  American corpus).
