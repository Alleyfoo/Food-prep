# Follow-up: benchmark, purpose-specific discovery and the existing sensory model

After R1's report, the review asked for three investigations before another
implementation round:

1. Can the model recover established preparations without being told the
   answers?
2. Does grouping by culinary function beat a global ranking?
3. Can the existing sensory model (transformation effects, Taste Circle
   properties) inform discovery, instead of a new flavour model?

The fourth ask, cooking the experiments, is the user's. Numbers below come
from `benchmark-results.md`, generated by `benchmark.py` from `benchmark.yaml`.

## The benchmark

42 established preparations, defined as a starting part and an operation
sequence:

- 25 from the web check of existing practice (`sources.md`);
- 17 from curated transformations and classical references.

`benchmark.py` applies each sequence, identifies the final material, and looks
for it in the generator's output.

**What "not told the answers" can and cannot mean here:**

| Leak | Removed? |
| --- | --- |
| `established_on` in operations.yaml was filled partly from the same web check, and the ranking uses it | **Yes.** Every variant except A ranks with `use_established=False` |
| The benchmark items set weights or rules | **Yes.** No weight or rule was tuned on the benchmark; the variants were defined before the runs (F and G after C showed the failure, see below) |
| The property values and requirements were written by an author who knew these preparations | **No.** It cannot be removed, only named. The benchmark measures recovery by a model built with this knowledge, not discovery from nothing |
| R1's 14 rule corrections were made while reading generator output that included some of these preparations | **No.** The same caveat |

## Results

**Generation:** 41 of 42 items are generated.

- The miss is expected: pickled avocado needs firm, underripe fruit, and the
  model has no ripeness property.
- A second miss, sautéed whole broccoli leaves, was a model gap: whole leaves
  "had to be cut first". It is fixed; leaves now wilt whole.

**Recovery within a short list.** Every variant shows the same number of
sequences as its purpose-specific counterpart (about 50–110 per ingredient,
against 2,000–9,000 ranked):

| Variant | Found | Share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| A global ranking, as shipped | 0 / 41 | 0% |
| B global, without `established_on` | 0 | 0% |
| C per function, ordered by global score | 3 | 7% |
| D global + sensory term | 0 | 0% |
| E per function by score + sensory | 2 | 5% |
| **F per function, simplest first** | **14** | **34%** |
| **G per function, simplest first + sensory** | **17** | **41%** |
| H control: global B at F's length | 0 | 0% |
| I control: global D at G's length | 1 | 2% |

**What it means:**

- **The global score does not recover established practice at all.** It
  rewards materials that serve many functions: crumbs, powders, broths.
  Established preparations usually do one thing well. Removing
  `established_on` changes almost nothing, so the established lists were not
  propping the score up.
- **Grouping by function alone barely helps (C, 7%)** while each list is
  still ordered by that score.
- **Grouping by function and ordering each list by simplicity works** (F,
  34%; G, 41%). Simplicity means fewest steps, then least effort, special
  equipment and conditions. At the same length the global ranking finds 0–1
  (H, I). Within a purpose, the simplest route is usually the established
  one: noodles from a stem are "ribbons", acid on a stem is "quick-pickle"
  or "ferment", crisp leaves are "roast", "dry", "deep-fry". That matches
  how cooks think: what do I want it to be, then what is the easiest way.

**Still missed, and why:**

| Missed | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Glazed carrots, tomato sauce, concassé, avocado mayonnaise, puréed broccoli, stir-fried stems | **Unranked.** Their last step adds no function the model can name |
| Pickled and fermented peeled stems (3 steps) | Simpler 1–2 step routes to the same function fill the 3 places |
| Grilled avocado wedges | The simplest flavour-shift route is grilling the whole flesh; the cut wedge is a different material by the model's identity rule |

## The existing sensory model

Two parts of the model already describe what techniques do to taste and
texture:

- **Transformation effects.** Each of the 66 curated transformations records
  `tags_after`: flavour and texture gains such as concentrated, savoury,
  smoky, tender-crisp.
- **Taste Circle properties.** Each curated state's `state_profile.flavour`
  lists the flavour dimensions it provides; the Taste Circle draws exactly
  these.

**How they are reused, with no new vocabulary:**
`operations.technique_effects()` aggregates both per technique, counting an
effect when at least half of that technique's curated transformations show
it. A generated path's operations link to techniques (`technique:`), so a
path inherits their effects:

- `sensory_gains()`: tags such as `flavour:smoky`;
- `taste_dimensions()`: Taste Circle dimensions such as `nutty_toasted`.

With `sensory=True` a gain counts as the function `flavour_shift`, and as a
ranking term.

| Technique | Curated transformations | Gains (≥ half) | Taste Circle dimensions |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| roast | 6 | concentrated, savory, sweet | nutty_toasted, sweet, umami |
| char | 1 | smoky, sweet | nutty_toasted, sweet, umami |
| reduce | 1 | concentrated, umami, dense | nutty_toasted, sweet, umami |
| steam | 1 | fresh, sweet, vegetal, tender-crisp | fresh_green, sweet |
| pickle | 4 | acidic, sharp, sweet-sour | aromatic, sour, sweet |
| stir_fry | 3 | savory, sweet, vegetal, firm | fresh_green, sweet, umami |

**What it does:**

- It makes preparations visible that had no structural function: steamed
  broccoli, grilled avocado, sautéed courgette. Broccoli's ranked set grows
  from 5,090 to 8,917.
- With simplest-first lists it adds three found items (F 14 → G 17).
- With score-ordered lists it does nothing or slightly harms (C 3 → E 2), and
  globally nothing (B 0 → D 0).
- **Useful for coverage, not a fix for ranking.**

**Limits of the reuse:**

- Effects are only as wide as the curated transformations. Char, reduce,
  steam and fry each rest on one transformation; cutting, pressing and most
  of the new operations have none, because no curated transformation uses
  them.
- An effect is the technique's typical gain across ingredients, not this
  material's. Roast's "sweet" holds for carrot more than for tomato skin.
- The Taste Circle dimensions are reported but not yet used in ranking.
  Matching them against a state's missing dimensions would connect discovery
  to plate repair; that is the natural next step.

## Recommendation for the next round

1. **Make purpose-specific, simplest-first lists the default view** of
   `foodprep explore`, instead of the global ranking. It is available now as
   `foodprep explore <ingredient> --by-function` but is not the default; that
   decision is the user's. Keep the global score only as a tie-breaker, or
   retire it.
2. **Keep the sensory term**, from the curated transformations, as a function
   (`flavour_shift`) rather than a weight. Widen it by curating
   transformations for the new ingredients (carrot, courgette): that also
   widens the evidence.
3. **Close the unranked gap** for refinements that do the same job better:
   the glaze over the reduction, the sauce over simmered flesh. The cleanest
   route is the sensory gains of the last step (already partly done), plus
   texture effects for pressing and sieving, which have no curated
   transformation yet.
4. **Use the Taste Circle dimensions next:** rank a function's options by how
   well they fill the missing dimensions of the dish they are for.
5. **Still first:** cook B-1, Z-1, Z-2 and B-3. The benchmark measures
   recovery of known practice. Only tasting can say whether the open ideas
   are worth making again.
